# 10 Documenting

In a maker ecosystem, documenting process and projects isn't just record-keeping — it's storytelling. Live exhibition events like Maker Faire serve a dual purpose: they create a meaningful deadline that focuses energy and effort, and a celebratory moment to pause, reflect, and share what's been made. But once the event is over or a project is dismantled, documentation becomes the only way members can show what they built and how they built it. Encouraging members to collect evidence of their process — from early sketches and brainstorming notes to photos and reflections — captures not just the finished work, but the thinking behind it. Assigning one team member per project to take on a documentarian role alongside their making duties is a simple way to make this happen consistently. That documentation can then live in a portfolio, giving makers something lasting to carry forward long after the project itself is gone. 

**Forms of documentation**
The story of a project is as important as the project itself. Encourage members to choose documentation tools that feel natural to them and to start early.

_**Notebooks**_
are the simplest starting point. A maker's notebook is a place to sketch concepts, jot notes, and paste in inspiration as it comes. Low-tech, affordable, and entirely personal — members can bring them to exhibition events to walk visitors through their process.
Project Binders take that raw material and organize it. Three-ring binders are flexible enough to hold component spec sheets, printed photos, napkin sketches, and anything else collected along the way. Date everything — an accurate chronological record becomes more interesting and valuable over time.

_**Posters**_
offer a more formal way to present finished work, modeled on the kind of displays scientists and engineers use at professional conferences. They're a great post-event reflection tool, prompting members to articulate their vision, process, and conclusions.

_**Project Binders.**_
take raw notes and sketches and organize them into a coherent record. Flexible enough to hold anything — spec sheets, clippings, napkin sketches — they capture the full range of a project's development. Date everything you add; a chronological record becomes more revealing and valuable over time.

_**Photos and Video**_
are among the most powerful ways to capture making in action. Candid shots of the team at work, time-lapses of a project taking shape, and a short video trailer can communicate what a finished object alone never could. A shared photo library — through a platform like Flickr — gives your makerspace a living visual archive.

_**Blogs and Social Media**_
can extend documentation into community. When members share their process publicly, they contribute to a broader culture of teaching and learning — and often find an audience they didn't expect. Writing up a project for others to replicate deepens the maker's own understanding, and opens the door for collaboration beyond your walls. 

Regardless of the format, these questions can help frame the story of any project:

What was the vision, and what were you hoping to make?
What inspired the idea, and why did you choose it?
Has anyone else tackled something similar, or was this one of a kind?
What was harder than expected? What surprised you?
Were there any memorable failures — or unexpected breakthroughs?
What's next? What ideas are already taking shape?

Along with adding to your personal record of what the Makerspace has accomplished together, you can also share this documentation with us and we will consider it as a post on our active blog at **makezine.com** or in Make: magazine.

You made it! (Literally!) This chapter covers the steps you should take to keep the momentum going and also to give back to the network so that others can learn from your experiences.

_**Congratulating Makers**_
As soon as possible after the event, reach out to everyone who participated — makers, mentors, sponsors, and supporters — to acknowledge their contributions and express your appreciation. A specific note of thanks goes a long way: recognize the makers who exhibited, the mentors whose patience and skill helped bring projects to life, and anyone who helped with setup or logistics.
Give participants a few talking points for the conversations that will inevitably follow — how many projects were made, total hours invested, event attendance. People will ask how to get involved, and your makers are your best ambassadors.

_**Collecting Feedback**_
Within a week or two after the event, reach out to members, parents, mentors, and volunteers for feedback. An online survey tool like Google Forms or SurveyMonkey makes this easy and allows for anonymous responses.
Good questions to include:

What worked well — and what was frustrating?
What part of the program was most engaging or most fun?
If you could change one thing for next year, what would it be?
What did you learn or gain from the experience?
Do you plan to keep making?
Know someone who should hear about this program? Share their contact.

The goal is two-fold: to keep improving the program, and to gather the stories and data that help sustain it.

_**Debriefing as Makerspace Leader**_
After the season wraps, take time to capture what happened while it's fresh. Write up what your group did — a blog post, a short video, or even just a few notes on what worked, what didn't, and what you'd change. Pull together project documentation, organize photos, and make sure every project is represented somewhere on your site. When members don't see their work acknowledged, they notice.
Before everyone disperses, ask members what they wish they'd known at the start. Ask mentors and volunteers to update their role descriptions so next year's cohort can hit the ground running.
Sharing with the Makerspace Network
The broader maker community grows stronger when individual makerspaces share what they've learned. Consider contributing in a few ways:

Write it up. A short post or recap of your season — highlights, challenges, and outcomes — helps other makerspace leaders learn from your experience.
Share images and video. A short video documenting your season or a well-tagged photo collection gives others a window into your work and builds the collective record of what making looks like in practice.
Contribute to this playbook. This is intended to be a living document, shaped by the growing experience of the network. Send comments, anecdotes, or suggestions to contact@makerspace.com.
